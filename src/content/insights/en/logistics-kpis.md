Ask three managers for last month's on-time delivery and you may get three different numbers. One counts orders, another counts order lines; one measures against the date the customer asked for, another against the date the warehouse promised. None of them is wrong, but a KPI only helps when everyone uses the same definition.

## What makes a good logistics KPI

A good KPI has four things: a precise definition, a target, an owner, and a data source that everyone trusts. It should also lead to action: when it turns red, someone knows what to do next. A short list of well-defined measures beats a long list that nobody owns.

## OTIF: on time and in full

On time in full is the most demanding measure of delivery service, because an order has to pass two tests at once.

<p class="formula">OTIF = orders delivered on time and in full ÷ all orders<small>An order counts only if it arrived on the agreed date with every line and every unit ordered.</small></p>

Because both tests must be passed, OTIF is always lower than the on-time rate or the in-full rate on its own. If 92% of orders arrive on time and 90% arrive complete, OTIF may be around 85%, because the two kinds of failure overlap only partly.

Agree three details before you report it:

- **Which date.** The date the customer requested, or the date you confirmed? The requested date is stricter and closer to what the customer experiences.
- **What counts as on time.** The same day, or within a window? Many retailers apply a tight window and charge penalties outside it.
- **Orders or lines.** OTIF by order is stricter; OTIF by line shows where the problem sits.

## Fill rate: the warehouse view

Fill rate measures how much of the demand you could ship from stock on the first attempt. Unit fill rate counts units; line fill rate counts order lines. A low fill rate points at inventory policy or forecasting rather than at transport, which is why it belongs next to OTIF in the same review.

## Cost per order

Service measures need a cost measure beside them, or they quietly push towards more stock and faster, more expensive transport. Cost per order adds up warehouse handling, packing and transport for a period and divides the total by the orders shipped. Cost per unit or per pallet works the same way. Tracked by customer or channel, it becomes the starting point for a cost-to-serve analysis.

## From numbers to decisions

- **Keep the list short.** OTIF, fill rate, cost per order and one warehouse productivity measure are enough for a monthly review.
- **Show the trend and the reasons.** A failure code on every late or short order turns a percentage into a to-do list: stock-out, picking error, carrier delay, wrong address.
- **Break it down.** Report by customer, lane and carrier. An average of 95% can hide a key customer at 70%.

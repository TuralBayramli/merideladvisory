Most companies know their gross margin by product. Far fewer know their margin by customer after logistics. That gap matters, because two customers buying the same product at the same price can cost very different amounts to serve.

One orders full pallets once a month, collects from your warehouse and pays in 30 days. The other orders three cartons twice a week, needs a delivery slot, returns damaged goods and pays in 90 days. On the invoice they look identical. In your cost base they are not.

## What cost-to-serve measures

Cost-to-serve takes the costs that sit below gross margin and assigns them to the customers, products and channels that cause them. The usual candidates are:

- **Order handling:** customer service time, order entry, invoicing
- **Warehousing:** picking, packing and storage, driven by order lines and units
- **Transport:** freight cost per drop, per pallet or per kilometre
- **Returns and claims:** handling, write-offs and credit notes
- **Payment terms:** the cost of cash tied up in receivables

The aim is not accounting precision. It is to allocate each cost by the activity that really drives it, so the result changes decisions.

## How to build one

1. **Collect the transaction data.** Order lines from your ERP, freight invoices, warehouse activity and the customer master. Twelve months is usually enough.
2. **Pick a driver for each cost.** Order lines for picking, drops or pallets for transport, minutes for customer service. Keep it simple: a handful of drivers explains most of the cost.
3. **Allocate and reconcile.** Spread each cost pool over its driver and check that the total still matches the profit and loss statement.
4. **Rank the results.** Sort customers from most to least profitable and plot cumulative profit. In many businesses the curve climbs above total profit and then falls back, because the last group of customers erodes what the first group earned.

## What to do with it

The answer is rarely to drop customers. More often it is to change how you serve them:

- minimum order sizes or a small-order surcharge
- fewer, fuller deliveries instead of frequent part loads
- service tiers, where faster or more flexible delivery has a price
- payment terms linked to volume
- pricing reviews for the accounts at the bottom of the curve

A first cost-to-serve model can be built in a few weeks from data you already have. Its main value is that it replaces opinions about "difficult customers" with numbers everyone can agree on.

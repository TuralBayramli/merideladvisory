Transport is often one of the largest sources of a company's indirect emissions, and customers, banks and investors ask about it more every year. The good news is that freight emissions follow a clear, standard logic: how much was moved, how far, by which mode, and with how much energy.

## The basic calculation

At its simplest, emissions are transport activity multiplied by an emission intensity factor.

<p class="formula">Emissions (kg CO₂e) = tonne-kilometres × emission factor (kg CO₂e per tonne-kilometre)<small>Tonne-kilometres = weight in tonnes × distance in kilometres. A 20-tonne load moved 1,000 km is 20,000 tonne-kilometres.</small></p>

The emission factor depends on the mode, the vehicle, the fuel and how full the vehicle is. Results are given in CO₂e, so that all greenhouse gases count in one unit.

## The standards: ISO 14083 and the GLEC Framework

Two references make results comparable between companies:

- **ISO 14083,** published in 2023, is the international standard for quantifying and reporting greenhouse gas emissions from transport chains, including hubs such as warehouses and terminals.
- **The GLEC Framework** from Smart Freight Centre is the practical method for logistics. It is aligned with ISO 14083 and gives guidance and default values for each mode.

Both count emissions well-to-wheel: from producing and delivering the fuel as well as from burning it.

## Primary data or default values

The most accurate figures use primary data: the actual fuel or energy used by the carrier, shared across the shipments it moved. Where that is not available, modelled values or default emission factors for each mode can be used, and replaced later as carriers share better data. Start with defaults, label them clearly, and improve the data for the largest lanes first.

## Why the mode matters

Per tonne-kilometre, road transport typically emits several times more than rail or sea. Moving long-haul legs from road to rail, or combining sea and rail on routes such as China to Europe, can cut the emissions of a shipment substantially, often with a longer but acceptable transit time. Fuller vehicles, fewer empty kilometres and cleaner fuels reduce emissions within each mode.

## How to start

1. Collect a year of shipments: origin, destination, weight and mode.
2. Calculate distances for each mode, including the road legs to and from rail terminals and ports.
3. Apply emission factors, starting with default values from a recognised source, and note which ones you used.
4. Report emissions by lane, mode, customer and carrier, and find the largest sources.
5. Model reduction options, such as a shift to rail or better load factors, with their effect on cost and transit time.
